        Can the surveyors' sightlines still be seen?

        Between 1936 and 1962 the Ordnance Survey remeasured Great Britain by sighting angles between concrete trig pillars, so each pillar had to be visible from its neighbours.

        This map redraws those sightlines over a terrain model and a 2010–15 model of trees and buildings. Which views still clear, and which are now blocked?

        Bold coloured lines are 829 views the surveyors observed. The faint orange web is every pair of pillars the model says could see each other over the terrain.

        How this was made

        Then: the rays the surveyors observed

        Between 1936 and 1962 the Ordnance Survey re-triangulated Great Britain from a network of trig pillars. Its 1967 history lists the directions observed at each primary station. The bold lines are the 829 rays observed from both ends, and each is re-tested against modern elevation models:

        • Still clear (608): clear over the terrain and over the 2010–15 surface model.
        • Blocked by trees or buildings (50): clear over the terrain but not over the 2010–15 surface model.
        • Blocked by the terrain (35): not clear even over bare ground from station height. Most are in the eastern lowlands, where the surveyors raised their instruments on steel towers.
        • Station height not recorded (136): one end is usually a bolt on a church or water tower, with no recorded height, so the ray cannot be tested.

        Now: every pillar pair in view

        The faint web joins the 6,079 standing pillars in the OS trig archive. Sight height is the archive height of the flush bracket, raised to the pillar top (1.22 m above ground) plus 0.25 m for an instrument; other marks get 1.5 m above their recorded height.

        Every pair up to 250 km apart was tested against OS Terrain 50, a 50 m bare-earth model, allowing for the Earth's curvature and a refraction coefficient of 0.13 and ignoring 100 m at each end: 230,499 pairs are clear. Each was then re-tested on the Copernicus GLO-30 surface model, a 30 m model that includes trees and buildings as radar saw them in 2010–15: 201,232 are still clear. The rest are drawn dashed.

        As a check, the model finds 612 of the 641 rays observed between two pillars (95.5%). This checks that real sightlines are found, not that every modelled line is real. Most of the misses are lowland rays in the eastern counties, where towers were used.

        Limits

        • These are models, not observations. None of the modern verdicts has been checked on the ground.
        • The surface model is 2010–15 radar. It misses hedgerows and thin cover, and cover may have changed since.
        • Lines that clear by a metre or two could go either way. In two test areas, resampling the surface model differently changed the verdict on 0.2–1.4% of lines, mostly ones clearing by less than 1 m.
        • Refraction varies with the weather. Deerness to Fair Isle was observed although the model, at 0.13, says it misses by 1.7 m.
        • Positions are converted without the OSTN15 grid, so they can be out by a metre or so.
